# Spoken Script Design

Writing for the ear is a working-memory problem. The listener cannot scan backward to recover a missing definition or untangle a sentence that introduced several relationships at once.

## The useful unit is a change in understanding

Treat the talk as a sequence of cognitive updates rather than a sequence of topics. A reliable local pattern is:

1. Start from something the listener already recognizes.
2. Add one important distinction, cause, or consequence.
3. Make it concrete with an example, image, or implication.
4. Let the result create the next useful question.

This is a default for concept-heavy explanation, not a mandatory prose template. A story may rely on chronology or discovery; a demonstration may rely on visible action. The invariant is that the listener should not have to hold several unresolved abstractions while waiting for the point.

## Opening contract

The opening has two jobs at once: it must give the speaker a clean entrance and give the audience a reason to stay. It often benefits from exact wording because neither side is yet in flow.

For a discovery-driven video, it also continues a promise that began before playback. Start from the working title and thumbnail, state the question they naturally open, and make the first spoken beat recognizably about that same question. Then add one reason the answer is more surprising or consequential than the packaging alone showed, and begin the first answer, example, scene, or mechanism. Read [packaging-and-opening.md](packaging-and-opening.md) when this alignment is part of the task.

A strong opening usually makes these legible in a natural order:

- the situation or tension the listener recognizes;
- the surprising consequence, unresolved question, or promised shift;
- why this matters now or to this audience;
- what the talk will help them understand;
- the first substantive step.

This does not require a formula or a “hook” voice. The opening can be calm, personal, analytical, or provocative. It only needs to make the subject and the reward of continuing clear before attention is spent.

## Audience questions, not section labels

An author may organize notes as definition, mechanism, history, consequences, and action. The audience experiences questions:

- What is happening to me?
- Why does this keep happening even when people are competent?
- What changes because of AI?
- What can I do differently?

Use whichever questions are genuinely alive in the current material. A good answer should make the next question feel necessary; if the sections can be rearranged without loss, the talk may still be an inventory rather than a progression.

## Cognitive reward and suspense

Keep attention with frequent rewards: a distinction becomes clear, a familiar experience gets a name, an example reveals a mechanism, or an implication changes what the listener notices.

Confusion is not suspense. Withholding the basic meaning of a sentence until several abstractions later creates cognitive debt. Suspense works after the audience understands what is at stake and wants the next answer.

## Spoken sentence audit

For a difficult passage, ask:

- What does the listener already know at the start of this sentence?
- How many new relationships must they retain before the sentence resolves?
- Can the main claim land first, with qualification or comparison following after it?
- Is an abstract label appearing before the concrete relation it names?
- Does the next sentence build on a stable idea, or repair an idea that never landed?

Long sentences can work when they unfold one relation. Short sentences can still fail when every line introduces a new abstraction.

## Memorable lines

A quotable line is strongest when the preceding reasoning has earned it. Place it where the listener has enough context to feel the compression. If the line is central to the speaker's voice or the video's later packaging, mark it as an exact-language anchor; otherwise let the speaker paraphrase naturally.

## Rehearsal check

Read the opening and one dense middle passage aloud, or simulate listening without looking back. Note where the meaning depends on rereading, where breath and thought boundaries diverge, and where the speaker must remember a clause that has not yet resolved. Revise the cognitive order before polishing rhythm.
